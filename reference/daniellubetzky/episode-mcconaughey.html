Episode #8

Matthew McConaughey

On Build or Break, the entrepreneurship podcast, Matthew McConaughey on his mom's Little Mr. Texas lie, 20 years of bad reviews and why he quit rom-coms.

0:00 / 0:00

Matthew
McConaughey

Subscribe and listen anywhere:

In 1977, eight-year-old Matthew McConaughey rode a horse, answered some questions, and walked off the stage holding a trophy while his mother told him he was Little Mr. Texas. The photo went on the family refrigerator, and every morning until he graduated high school she greeted him at breakfast the same way: "Look at you, Little Mr. Texas." About six years ago, going back through his life to write Greenlights, he zoomed in on the nameplate. It said runner-up. He had believed otherwise for more than 40 years, and he says he still kind of believes it.

On the eighth episode of Build or Break, Daniel Lubetzky's weekly entrepreneurship podcast, McConaughey tells him why he files that story under failure and success at the same time. His mother, now 94, is what he calls the queen of denial, and he argues that refusing to give a crisis credit is part of what made him resilient. The catch, which he says he only learned in his forties, is that getting up and dusting yourself off is not enough. If you never look at the pothole you fell in, you will keep stepping in it.

That leads to one of the strongest threads in the conversation: refusing to be a victim. McConaughey says the world will often tell people they should feel like victims when they don't, and that sometimes the right question about a failure is "by whose measure?" Daniel connects it to parenting, and McConaughey explains how he and Camila think about raising kids with a famous last name: kids aren't afraid of heights until they fall, so don't scare them at six feet, and teach them never to lead with entitlement but never to lower their heads either. He also makes the case for bringing embarrassment back.

Then the career. He met the right guy in a bar in Austin, got three lines in Dazed and Confused that turned into three weeks of work, and drove to Hollywood with $2,000 in a U-Haul. He got the first two roles he auditioned for. Offered a small part in A Time to Kill, he sat in Joel Schumacher's office in a sleeveless John Mellencamp T-shirt, asked who was playing the lead, and said, "I think I should." Schumacher laughed. Six weeks later the studio asked him to screen test for it.

He explains why he once took 20 years of his reviews into the desert for a weekend and read only the bad ones, and how he learned to tell a critic with a point from one who had it out for him. He explains why he walked away from romantic comedies ("I could do this tomorrow"), what it took to go deep enough into Rust Cohle for True Detective, and why he thinks chasing more money at all costs means failing in your character along the way. Mid-answer, his phone rings. It's his daughter, and he picks up, because she is one of the few calls that gets through.

The conversation ends on America. McConaughey breaks down the America 250 message Daniel calls mesmerizing, recorded in one take with no teleprompter: America as a bet that is still on the table, an idea that has never arrived, and a verb rather than a finished thing. Then comes the Mexican Inquisition, where he names the lawyer who worked for him for free before he had any money, and a rapid-fire round on aliens, impressions, tequila, Texas exceptionalism, and the confession that he invented the word McConaissance himself.

Key takeaways

  • Some failures are better left unknown. McConaughey believed he won Little Mr. Texas for more than 40 years before learning the trophy said runner-up, and he credits that belief with part of his confidence.
  • Resilience is only half of it. Getting up after every fall kept him going, but he says he only learned in his forties to look at the pothole, too, so he would stop stepping in the same one.
  • Ask "by whose measure?" before you call something a failure. The world is quick to tell people they failed or should feel like victims, and he argues you are allowed to keep your own scorecard.
  • Don't take away a child's "beauty of ignorance." Kids aren't afraid of heights until they fall, so he tries not to scare his kids at six feet, and he teaches them never to lead with entitlement and never to lower their heads.
  • Ask for what you want, even when you're nervous. Offered a small part in A Time to Kill, he told the director he should play the lead, and six weeks later he was screen testing for it.
  • Learn which critics to listen to. Reading 20 years of bad reviews in one weekend taught him to separate people who just don't like him from good critics who are right about where he came up short.
  • Pick work that scares you. He left rom-coms when he realized he could do them tomorrow, because he wants to sweat in his boots and come out having learned something.

Common questions

What is Build or Break?

Build or Break is a weekly entrepreneurship podcast hosted by Daniel Lubetzky, the founder of KIND Snacks and a Shark Tank investor. He talks with builders, from founders to actors, athletes and public figures, about the setbacks that shaped them. New episodes come out every Tuesday on YouTube, Spotify and Apple Podcasts.

Was Matthew McConaughey Little Mr. Texas?

Not quite. In 1977, at age eight, he competed for Little Mr. Texas, and his mother told him he had won. A photo of him with the trophy hung on the family refrigerator for years. About six years ago, while writing Greenlights, he zoomed in on the trophy's nameplate and saw that it said runner-up. He says he believed he had won for more than 40 years.

Why did Matthew McConaughey stop doing rom-coms?

He says the scripts started to feel like something he could do tomorrow. He wanted roles that scared him and made him sweat in his boots, so he would come out of each film having learned something. On the episode, Daniel notes that he turned down a $14.5 million offer along the way, and McConaughey says his last romantic comedy was Ghosts of Girlfriends Past.

How did Matthew McConaughey get his start in acting?

He met casting director Don Phillips in a bar in Austin, read for Dazed and Confused, and turned three lines into three weeks of work. After graduating, he drove to Hollywood with $2,000 and a U-Haul and got the first two roles he auditioned for. Offered a small part in A Time to Kill, he told director Joel Schumacher he should play the lead, and six weeks later he was asked to screen test for it.

How does Matthew McConaughey raise his kids?

He says kids aren't afraid of heights until they fall, so he tries not to scare them over small risks and take away what he calls the beauty of ignorance. Because people judge his kids by their famous last name, he and Camila teach them never to lead with entitlement, but never to lower their heads either.

What did Matthew McConaughey say in his America 250 message?

He described America as 250 years young: an idea and a bet that a self-governing people could be something worth becoming, with the bet still on the table. He told Daniel he recorded it in one take with no teleprompter, working from bullet points, and that he thinks of America as a verb, not a place we will ever finish arriving at.

About Matthew McConaughey

Matthew McConaughey is an Academy Award-winning actor, producer, author, and professor from Texas. His breakout role came in Dazed and Confused, followed by A Time to Kill, and later the run of roles known as the McConaissance, including Dallas Buyers Club and True Detective. He is the author of the bestselling memoir Greenlights and co-founder, with his wife Camila, of Pantalones Organic Tequila.
